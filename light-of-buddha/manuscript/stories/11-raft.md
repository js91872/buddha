# 11. The Raft Left on the Shore

![A traveller pauses beside a raft on the far bank: the teaching parable.](../../artwork/story-illustrations/11.jpg)

The water lay between the traveller and safety. On his own bank there was danger; on the opposite bank, the possibility of continuing his journey. There was no bridge. No ferryman came towards him. He could stand and wish for a crossing, or begin making one.

This was a traveller in a story the Buddha asked his listeners to imagine. The parable gives him no name or family history. What mattered was the difficulty before him, and what he would do with the means available.

He gathered what the bank offered: grass, branches, leaves and smaller lengths of wood. None was a boat in itself. Bound together, however, they could bear a person's weight. He worked them into a raft, something serviceable rather than handsome. The task required judgement. Too little material would fail him; a collection left unbound would scatter as soon as he entered the water.

Then he put the raft to use. He crossed by his own effort, moving his hands and feet, depending on what he had made. The Buddha's image contains both reliance and labour. The traveller did not reach safety merely by admiring his raft, declaring loyalty to it, or arguing that it was better than another traveller's vessel. He had to enter the water.

At last he reached the farther bank. Now the danger that had made the raft necessary lay behind him. He could feel gratitude for its usefulness. Without it, he might still be staring across the current, unable to proceed.

But gratitude can become a curious kind of burden. Suppose, the Buddha asked, the man decided that such an excellent raft ought never to leave him. He might lift it onto his head or strap it across his back and set out along the road. Branches that had supported him in water would catch against the undergrowth. What had made movement possible would make each subsequent step harder.

The listeners could see the mistake. A raft was made for crossing. Carrying it everywhere would misunderstand its kindness, if a bundle of wood could be called kind. The wiser traveller would leave it on dry ground or set it afloat, and continue without the weight.

The Buddha brought the image back to his teaching. The Dhamma also had a purpose: it was to help people cross beyond suffering. Learning it well mattered. Practising it mattered. Yet turning it into a possession to defend, a source of superiority, or an argument to carry into every meeting could prevent the very freedom it was meant to serve.

The lesson was not permission to discard discipline whenever it grew inconvenient. A traveller still in the current who threw his raft away would hardly be wise. The question was whether a means was being used for its proper work, and whether attachment had quietly taken the place of understanding.

On the farther bank, in the simple space of the parable, the man could finally straighten his back. The raft remained a good raft. He did not need to despise it in order to let it go. He could honour what had helped him by walking freely into the country it had enabled him to reach.

## Wisdom to Remember

A teaching fulfils its purpose through practice, not possession.

## A Reflection for Today

Consider one practice or idea that has helped you. Ask whether you are using it to become kinder and clearer, or to establish that you are right. Let gratitude remain, while loosening the need to turn help into identity.
