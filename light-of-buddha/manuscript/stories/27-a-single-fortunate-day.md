# 27. A Single Fortunate Day

![A monk sits attentive by a still pool in the present dawn.](../../artwork/story-illustrations/27.jpg)

A day could be spent in places the body had never gone.

Someone seated beneath a tree might return inwardly to a long-vanished season, rearranging its details, wishing to inhabit an earlier self. Another might live among imagined conditions not yet arrived, waiting for a future body, a future feeling, a future life to make the present one complete.

At Jeta's Grove, near Savatthi, the Buddha gave the monks a teaching about a life no longer carried away in those directions. He began in verse, then explained its meaning. Its traditional title is difficult to render precisely, but its concern is clear: how to use this available day and night with understanding.

The past had already gone. The future had not yet come. What had arisen in the present could be known clearly, without being swept away by it. The work called for diligence now, because no one could bargain for the certainty of tomorrow.

That teaching might sound, at first, like an instruction simply to stop remembering or planning. The Buddha's explanation was more exact. He described being drawn into the past through delight in what one had been: a particular body, certain feelings, perceptions, intentions, or states of consciousness. The mind gathered around these remembered experiences and made a home there.

The future could attract the same movement. A person imagined becoming someone with a certain appearance, certain pleasures, certain experiences. Desire reached towards the picture and began to inhabit it, although the day itself had not arrived.

The problem was not the bare existence of memory or foresight. It was the delighted clinging that carried a person away.

Yet simply remaining interested in the present did not complete the instruction. The present could also become a place of attachment. Body and feeling, perception and intention, consciousness itself could be mistaken for an enduring self, or treated as the possessions or dwelling place of such a self.

The Buddha described the instructed disciple as knowing these experiences without making those identifications. Attention belonged together with insight. A passing feeling did not have to become a definition of the whole person. A thought did not have to become a permanent address.

The teaching thus asked for something subtler than enjoying whatever happened next. One could be fully occupied by present pleasure and still be entangled. One could notice each sensation and still insist that it confirmed an unchanging identity. Clear seeing included learning how experience was being claimed.

Then the verse returned. The listeners heard again the urgency of practising while the opportunity was available. Its mention of death did not reveal a secret timetable. It reminded them that delay could not make an agreement with mortality.

The discourse records no dramatic interruption and no named listener whose life changed before sunset. Its scene is a teacher giving instruction and disciples listening. The drama belongs to the ordinary mind, which can spend so much of its brief life elsewhere.

A fortunate day, in this account, need not be free from difficulty. It need not produce an event worth telling the whole village. Its value lies in the care with which experience is understood and the persistence with which the path is cultivated.

The day already at hand was enough to begin.

## Wisdom to Remember

Use the present with clear understanding, without turning the past, the future, or this passing moment into something to cling to.

## A Reflection for Today

When a memory or imagined future captures your attention, name it gently. Return to one useful action available now. Planning and remembering remain valuable; notice when they cease to guide your life and begin to replace it.
