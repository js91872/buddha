# 25. Victory Without Peace

![Pasenadi and weary soldiers return after defeat.](../../artwork/story-illustrations/25.jpg)

The army came home, and the king came home with it. Victory did not.

Ajatasattu, ruler of Magadha, had raised an army and advanced towards Kasi, against King Pasenadi of Kosala. Pasenadi heard of the movement and gathered his own forces. The two kings met in battle. Ajatasattu prevailed, and the defeated Pasenadi withdrew to his capital at Savatthi.

The canonical account gives the event with little ornament. It does not supply the noise of the fighting, the words exchanged before it, or a count of the dead. Its brevity leaves the defeat starkly visible: a king had gone out to contest a territory and had returned unable to secure it.

In Savatthi, the event became news that travelled with the ordinary movements of the day. Monks entered the city in the morning carrying their bowls. They went from household to household for food, close enough to civic life to hear what had happened. After eating and returning from their alms round, they approached the Buddha and reported the battle.

Their account named the kings and described the outcome. An army had attacked; another had answered; one ruler had won and the other had fallen back. It was the kind of report that could invite an immediate judgement about strength, strategy, or the changing fortune of kingdoms.

The Buddha spoke first about the company the kings kept. Ajatasattu had harmful friends and companions, he said, whereas Pasenadi had good ones. Yet the presence of good companions did not erase the immediate suffering of military defeat. That night Pasenadi would rest badly.

The observation allowed no easy equation between goodness and worldly success. A person could have good friends and still lose a battle. Someone surrounded by bad counsel could still win one. The result on the field did not settle the deeper question of how a life should be lived.

Then the Buddha turned from the two kings to the wider pattern. Winning gave rise to hostility. Losing brought distress. There was ease for those who had become peaceful and relinquished the contest between victory and defeat.

The teaching reached beyond the king who had retreated. The victor's position was not the same as peace. His success existed in a relationship with another person's humiliation, and that relationship could keep hostility alive. To have won did not mean that the cause of conflict had ended.

Pasenadi's unrest that night belongs to the text. We need not invent what he thought as he lay down. The account does not give his private regrets, his plans, or any reply he made to the teaching. It also does not end by awarding him a compensating triumph.

Instead, it leaves the reader with a difficult possibility. One may prevail and remain bound to rivalry. One may lose and be consumed by it. To become free of the contest requires an understanding that the contest itself cannot provide.

The monks had brought news of which king held the advantage. They received a teaching about why neither outcome, by itself, could give the heart its rest. Beyond the defeated ruler's troubled night lay another kind of accomplishment: learning how to live without making an enemy's defeat the condition of one's own peace.

## Wisdom to Remember

Success does not automatically end hostility. Peace asks what we are cultivating in the struggle to prevail.

## A Reflection for Today

Recall a disagreement you are still trying to win. Identify the practical issue that needs resolution, then distinguish it from the desire to humiliate the other person. A firm boundary or fair remedy can be pursued without feeding that desire.
