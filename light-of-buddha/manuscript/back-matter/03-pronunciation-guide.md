# A Gentle Guide to Pronunciation

Pronunciation varies across Buddhist communities. These English approximations are aids to reading, not a substitute for learning from a knowledgeable teacher or listening to a community’s chanting. Pali has no single exclusive national script; the Roman letters here are a transliteration.

Say **a** approximately as the first vowel in “about”, **i** as in “sit”, and **u** as in “put”, with regional English differences in mind. A bar marks length: **ā**, **ī**, **ū** are held longer, approximately “ah”, “ee”, “oo”. **E** and **o** are generally pure vowels rather than English glides. Pronounce each syllable clearly.

**C** resembles “ch” in “church”; **j** resembles “j” in “joy”; **ñ** resembles “ny” in “canyon”; **ṅ** is the “ng” in “sing”. **Ṃ** indicates a nasal sound whose realisation varies with context and chanting tradition. A dot under **ṭ**, **ḍ**, **ṇ**, or **ḷ** indicates a sound made with the tongue drawn farther back than its undotted counterpart.

In **kh**, **gh**, **ch**, **jh**, **ṭh**, **ḍh**, **th**, **dh**, **ph** and **bh**, the h indicates an accompanying breath. Thus **th** is not the English sound in “thin”, and **ph** is not “f”. Doubled consonants are given additional duration.

Approximate reading aids: **Buddha**, BOOD-dha, with a light breath after d; **Dhamma**, DHAM-ma; **Saṅgha**, SANG-gha; **mettā**, MET-taa; **nibbāna**, nib-BAA-na; **saraṇa**, sa-ra-na, with the n made farther back. These capital letters help English readers find a rhythm; they do not prescribe a universal Pali stress rule.

For the refuge phrase, take it slowly: **Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi** — bud-dhaṃ / sa-ra-ṇaṃ / gac-chā-mi. Give the long ā time to sound. A respectful, unhurried recitation matters more than imitating an imagined exotic accent.
