# Historical setting and art references

Accessed live through web research, 23 October 2026. These records inform interpretation only. No museum photograph or artwork is reproduced in the book.

## Directly inspected museum and Indian archive evidence

1. Metropolitan Museum of Art, [Seated Buddha, 2003.593.1](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/72381), Gandhara, **1st to mid-2nd century CE**, bronze with traces of gold leaf. Curatorial discussion describes a naturalistic garment treatment shaped by classical contacts, and the development and codification of Buddha gestures over later centuries. Its own audio transcript uses “about early second century”; the catalogue’s wider dating is retained here. This is centuries after Gautama’s lifetime, so neither the hair nor drapery is a contemporary portrait record. The catalogue labels its image Public Domain, but no image was downloaded for reuse.

2. Metropolitan Museum of Art, [Buddha Shakyamuni, 2012.458](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/78191), Central Tibet, **12th century**, brass with pigments. The curatorial note explains extended earlobes, urna (forehead hair-mark), ushnisha (cranial protuberance), meditation posture and earth-touch gesture. These are later devotional conventions and textual auspicious marks. This Tibetan object's particular details must not be passed off as eyewitness evidence for ancient north Indian dress. Image is marked Public Domain; not reproduced.

3. National Museum, New Delhi, accession 47.47, [Standing Buddha](https://museumsofindia.gov.in/repository/record/nat_del-47-47-306), Nalanda, Pala dynasty, **10th century CE**, bronze, lost-wax process. The Indian national museum portal describes a sanghati covering both shoulders, curled hair, ushnisha, extended earlobes and urna. The record describes a later Pala devotional image, not a fifth-century BCE reconstruction. Portal footer states All Rights Reserved; no commercial image reuse is assumed or undertaken.

## Design application and limits

The illustrations are historically informed devotional interpretations. They are not archaeological reconstructions and should be described that way. Traditional curls, ushnisha and urna on the cover are deliberate later iconographic conventions that help readers recognise Gautama Buddha. Their presence does not establish his recorded appearance. Do not attach a Tibetan flame finial, Chinese pagoda, Japanese temple gate, later monumental stupa or modern Buddhist national dress to an early Indian episode without an explicit artistic reason and disclosure.

Pali narrative sources give direct literary cues for simple robes and bowls, forest groves, bamboo woodland, potter’s sheds, towns, ploughs, rice meals and the vina. These are stronger scene anchors than imported palace fantasy. Exact garment cuts, dye shades, bowl materials, building plans, landscape vegetation, hairstyles and physical appearances are incompletely documented for these particular events. Choose restrained earthen construction, unstitched-looking cloth and regionally plausible plants as artistic inference, not verified facts. Avoid decorative Sanskrit or Pali inscriptions unless separately verified. Bodhi foliage should have heart-shaped leaves with long tapered tips; it must not become a generic maple canopy. Lotus motifs are devotional composition elements rather than proof that every grove had a lotus pond.

The art does not imitate a living artist’s signature style. It may use broad historical media categories—watercolour, ink, gouache, classical oil handling—without claiming an ancient artifact or genuine historical painting was discovered. Inspect all rendered mudras, fingers, knees, bowl grips and clothing folds; literary-source accuracy cannot guarantee generated-image anatomy.
